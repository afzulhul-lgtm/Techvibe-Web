Airbus fello'fly Project: Commercial Formation Flight Tested

By James Carter Senior Editor
Published: May 21, 2026 Updated: May 21, 2026 6 Min Read

Executing a transatlantic demonstration in late 2021, Airbus validated the aerodynamic viability of the "fello'fly" project by flying two A350 aircraft in close formation from Toulouse to Montreal. Rather than waiting for next-generation propulsion systems to mature, Airbus engineers are manipulating existing aerodynamic physics to capture wake energy, directly lowering long-haul fuel consumption for current wide-body fleets.

Commercial aviation typically mandates strict 5-to-10-nautical-mile horizontal separation rules in oceanic airspace, rendering formation flight a practice strictly reserved for military operations. However, by deploying specialized autonomous flight controls and vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) datalinks, Airbus proved that maintaining a 1.5-nautical-mile distance between two 280-ton passenger jets at cruising altitude is both mechanically stable and highly efficient.

Wake Energy Retrieval Aerodynamics

Every fixed-wing aircraft generates lift, which consequently produces a rotating vortex of air spilling off each wingtip. By positioning a follower aircraft precisely 1.5 to 2 nautical miles (roughly 3 kilometers) behind the leader, the trailing jet can ride the smooth, upward-moving air of this vortex—a concept known as Wake Energy Retrieval (WER).

This physical updraft reduces the engine thrust required by the trailing aircraft to maintain Mach 0.85 cruising speeds. Biological systems, such as migrating geese, have relied on this V-formation efficiency for millennia. Airbus has successfully translated this phenomenon into a commercial aerospace context, establishing that the A350 can sustain this aerodynamic link for extended oceanic crossings.

Autonomous Flight Control Integration

Holding a 1.5-nautical-mile separation at 35,000 feet requires precision positioning that exceeds manual human piloting limits. For the fello'fly project, Airbus engineered specific flight control system upgrades to automate the trailing aircraft's proximity to the leading jet.

The system relies heavily on Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) data combined with a secure V2V datalink broadcasting telemetry in real-time. The follower aircraft's autopilot continuously calculates the trajectory of the leader's wake vortex, autonomously adjusting thrust and control surfaces to remain latched in the updraft while avoiding the turbulent core. During this phase, pilots transition into system monitors, ensuring the automation functions within safety parameters.

Fuel Efficiency and Emissions Data

Data gathered from the transatlantic flight tests confirmed a direct fuel consumption reduction of 5% for the follower aircraft. On a standard 10-hour intercontinental route, a 5% reduction equates to saving roughly 3 to 4 metric tons of Jet A-1 fuel.

This fuel savings translates directly to preventing up to 12 metric tons of CO2 from being released into the upper atmosphere per paired flight. However, an operational limitation exists: the efficiency gain strictly benefits the trailing aircraft. The lead aircraft continues to burn fuel at standard rates. To yield net network benefits, airlines will need to schedule flights originating from different airports to converge strategically along high-density routes, such as the North Atlantic Tracks.

Key Takeaways

  • Airbus executed commercial formation flight tests using two A350 aircraft maintaining a 1.5-nautical-mile separation.
  • Wake Energy Retrieval (WER) allows the trailing aircraft to reduce thrust, cutting fuel burn by an average of 5%.
  • The procedure requires specialized V2V datalinks and autonomous GNSS flight controls to remain safely within the updraft at Mach 0.85.
  • Current ICAO separation regulations present the primary barrier, requiring airlines and Air Navigation Service Providers (ANSPs) to update tracking protocols.

Regulatory Hurdles and ATC Implementation

The technological hardware is functional, but international aviation law restricts immediate commercial deployment. The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) enforces minimum horizontal separation distances that currently prohibit jets from maintaining a 1.5-nautical-mile spacing outside of specialized testing zones.

Airbus must collaborate directly with Air Navigation Service Providers (ANSPs), such as NATS in the UK and DSNA in France, to rewrite Air Traffic Management (ATM) rules. The proposed operational concept requires controllers to clear the paired jets as a single entity on radar screens. Widespread adoption relies entirely on regulators approving these "cleared as one" flight plans for standard oceanic airspace.

Actionable Conclusion

Airlines operating dense international networks must evaluate their current wide-body fleets for V2V datalink upgrade compatibility. Fello'fly presents a verifiable, near-term method to cut fuel expenditures and hit carbon-reduction targets without waiting for the scalability of hydrogen propulsion or sustainable aviation fuels (SAF). Airline executives should actively engage with ICAO working groups to accelerate the Air Traffic Control regulatory adjustments necessary to make commercial formation flight standard practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

The fello'fly project is an Airbus research initiative that tests Wake Energy Retrieval (WER) by flying a commercial passenger jet approximately 1.5 nautical miles behind a lead aircraft to reduce aerodynamic drag.

How much fuel can be saved by formation flight?

During transatlantic flight tests using A350 aircraft, the trailing aircraft achieved a direct fuel consumption reduction of 5%, saving approximately 3 to 4 metric tons of fuel on a standard 10-hour flight.

Airbus engineered specific autonomous flight control systems and Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) V2V datalinks to lock the trailing aircraft into the precise updraft zone, avoiding the turbulent vortex core while maintaining a stable 1.5-nautical-mile separation.

When will this be available for commercial airlines?

Widespread commercial deployment is currently restricted by International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) separation regulations. Implementation requires rewriting Air Traffic Management protocols to treat paired flights as a single radar entity.
