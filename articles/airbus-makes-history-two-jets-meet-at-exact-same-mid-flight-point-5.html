Airbus Makes History: Two Jets Meet at Exact Same Mid Flight Point

By James Carter Senior Editor
Published: May 21, 2026 Updated: May 21, 2026 6 Min Read

In a significant breakthrough for aviation sustainability, Airbus has successfully completed a series of flight tests demonstrating the viability of formation flight for commercial airliners. This initiative, known as the "fello'fly" project, explores how two passenger jets can travel safely in close proximity—specifically by utilizing the wake energy generated by the lead aircraft—to achieve remarkable improvements in fuel efficiency and carbon emission reduction.

While the concept of formation flying is common among military aircraft, applying it to large commercial jets has historically been considered too complex due to safety, navigational, and airspace management challenges. However, recent flight tests using two A350 aircraft have proven that with the right technology, this practice could become a staple of long-haul travel.

The Science of Wake Energy Retrieval

At the core of this milestone is a concept known as "Wake Energy Retrieval" (WER). When an aircraft flies, its wings generate lift, creating a vortex of air at the wingtips. This wake creates a region of upward-moving air—or "uplift"—behind the aircraft. By positioning a second aircraft precisely within this wake, the follower jet can use this naturally occurring energy to reduce its own drag.

Think of it as similar to how migrating birds fly in a V-formation. By positioning themselves in the wake of the lead bird, the follower saves significant energy. Airbus has translated this biological phenomenon into aviation physics, successfully testing it with two massive A350 jetliners flying safely in a longitudinal formation.

How Airbus Syncs the Aircraft

Precision is everything. For the follower aircraft to benefit from the wake energy, it must stay in a very specific position relative to the leader. Manual control for such prolonged, high-precision flight would be incredibly fatiguing for pilots. Consequently, Airbus utilizes enhanced flight control and navigation systems that allow the follower aircraft to "latch on" to the leader's wake via autopilot.

This automated system relies on advanced satellite navigation and real-time communication between the two jets. The system ensures the follower maintains the ideal distance and alignment, providing a safe, consistent ride while the pilots monitor the systems. This integration makes the flight path as predictable and safe as standard commercial cruising.

Efficiency Gains and Environmental Impact

The numbers from the fello'fly tests are compelling. By flying in formation, the follower aircraft can achieve fuel savings ranging from 5% to 10% on a typical long-haul cruise segment. When scaled across the global fleet, this could lead to millions of tons of carbon dioxide emissions being avoided annually.

As the aviation industry faces growing pressure to achieve "net-zero" goals, technologies that do not require building entirely new planes are invaluable. Fello'fly offers an immediate way to upgrade the efficiency of existing wide-body fleets without requiring massive engine redesigns.

Key Takeaways

  • Airbus's fello'fly project uses Wake Energy Retrieval to allow jets to fly in a fuel-efficient formation.
  • The system uses advanced autopilot and satellite navigation to keep jets at a safe, precise distance.
  • Testing shows potential fuel consumption reductions of 5% to 10%, directly lowering carbon emissions.

Challenges and Future Regulation

Despite the success of the tests, implementation is not happening tomorrow. The primary hurdles are currently regulatory. Air traffic control systems are built on the assumption that aircraft maintain specific, wide vertical and horizontal separation distances at all times. Introducing formation flight requires a complete overhaul of how flight paths and separation minimums are handled.

Furthermore, air traffic controllers will need specialized training and tools to manage these "paired" flights without disrupting other traffic flows. Airbus is working closely with air navigation service providers (ANSPs) and international regulators like ICAO to draft the safety protocols that would eventually allow commercial airlines to adopt this technology on everyday routes.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Airbus fello'fly project?

The fello'fly project is an Airbus research initiative that tests formation flight, where a follower aircraft flies in the wake of a lead aircraft to save fuel and reduce CO2 emissions.

How much fuel can be saved by formation flight?

By utilizing wake energy retrieval (flying in the uplift of the lead plane's wingtip vortex), the follower aircraft can achieve fuel savings of 5% to 10% on long-haul flights.

Is it safe to fly two commercial jets so close together?

Yes, Airbus uses advanced flight control systems and satellite-based navigation that allow the follower aircraft to maintain a safe, steady position automatically, ensuring safety similar to standard commercial cruise flight.

When will this be available for commercial airlines?

While flight tests have been successful, implementation requires changes to international aviation regulations and air traffic control procedures before it can be used on regular commercial routes.
