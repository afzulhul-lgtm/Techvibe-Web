The World’s Longest Underwater High-Speed Train Revealed: Linking Two Continents Beneath the Sea

By Mike Reynolds Senior Editor
Published: May 21, 2026 Updated: May 21, 2026 6 Min Read

On March 22, 2026, transportation authorities from Spain and Morocco formalized the next planning phase for the Strait of Gibraltar tunnel, a high-speed rail link connecting Europe and Africa. First proposed in 1979, the project secured renewed funding and engineering commitments ahead of the 2030 FIFA World Cup, which will be jointly hosted by Spain, Portugal, and Morocco. Rather than a fictional transatlantic tube connecting New York and London, this 24.2-mile (38.9-kilometer) infrastructure initiative represents the actual future of intercontinental underwater travel.

Simultaneously, China is advancing the Bohai Strait tunnel, a 76-mile (123-kilometer) underwater rail project connecting the cities of Dalian and Yantai. While the Gibraltar tunnel claims the title for directly linking two distinct continents, the Bohai project will secure the record for the absolute longest underwater high-speed train route on the planet. Both initiatives utilize highly advanced tunnel boring machines (TBMs) and atmospheric pressure regulation systems to overcome the extreme hydrostatic pressure and seismic activity inherent to deep-sea engineering.

The Gibraltar Project: Linking Europe and Africa

The Strait of Gibraltar tunnel is engineered to span 24.2 miles between Punta Paloma, Spain, and Punta Malabata, Morocco, with 17.2 miles (27.7 kilometers) submerged beneath the convergence of the Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean Sea. The structural design specifies twin single-track rail tunnels flanked by a central service and emergency evacuation tunnel, closely mirroring the proven architecture of the English Channel Tunnel. Passenger trains on this route are designed to operate at speeds up to 186 mph (300 km/h), reducing the total land transit time between Madrid and Casablanca to under five hours.

A primary engineering limitation of the Gibraltar route involves the extreme depth of the strait. To avoid the deepest oceanic trenches in the region, engineers mapped a curved, U-shaped path that limits the maximum tunnel depth to 1,550 feet (475 meters) below sea level. Operating high-speed rail at this depth requires specialized ventilation shafts and precise atmospheric pressure regulation within the train cabins. Without these systems, passengers would experience severe barotrauma during the rapid descents and ascents required to enter and exit the underwater sections at high speeds.

The Reality of the "Longest" Title: China's Bohai Strait

While the Gibraltar project links continents, China's Bohai Strait Tunnel holds the definitive title for the longest planned underwater rail corridor. With an estimated construction budget of $36 billion, this 76.4-mile structure connects the Liaodong and Shandong peninsulas across the Bohai Sea. When operational, the tunnel will condense a standard 8-hour maritime ferry crossing into a 40-minute high-speed train ride, operating at speeds of 155 mph (250 km/h).

The Bohai project faces distinct geographical challenges compared to its European counterpart. The planned tunnel path intersects the Tan-Lu fault zone, a highly active seismic belt known for historical tectonic instability. To mitigate earthquake risks, the Chinese Railway Engineering Corporation is deploying flexible tunnel lining joints and isolated suspension systems for the track beds. Despite these advanced technological safeguards, the inherent risk of a high-magnitude seismic event occurring directly above a submerged train traveling at high speeds remains a critical and highly scrutinized vulnerability in the project's risk assessment.

The comparative data illustrates a distinct shift in operational capabilities over the past three decades. While the 1994 Channel Tunnel currently limits passenger trains to 99 mph (160 km/h) due to severe aerodynamic drag and thermal heat generation limits within the narrow tunnel shaft, modern environmental control systems allow the Gibraltar and Bohai tunnels to support sustained speeds up to 186 mph safely. The Marmaray Tunnel, the first rail link to connect Europe and Asia, remains relatively slow but proves the viability of intercontinental passenger rail.

Engineering Challenges and Seismic Limitations

Drilling beneath the ocean floor presents extreme hydrostatic challenges. At 475 meters depth, the water pressure in the Strait of Gibraltar is approximately 47 atmospheres, requiring specialized tunnel boring machines capable of withstanding over 680 pounds per square inch of external force. The geological composition of the strait complicates matters further; the seabed features highly permeable breccia flysch, loose clay, and porous limestone, all of which are highly prone to sudden and catastrophic water ingress during the boring phase.

To secure the structural integrity of the shafts, engineering crews must utilize continuous pre-grouting techniques, injecting specialized cement mixtures into the rock face ahead of the drill bit to seal fissures. This process drastically slows the rate of advance, limiting the boring machines to a few dozen feet per day. The sheer volume of exhaust fumes, displaced seawater, and excavated rock necessitates the construction of artificial islands and deep-sea ventilation shafts to maintain a safe working environment for the subterranean crews.

Key Takeaways

  • The Strait of Gibraltar Tunnel is a planned 24.2-mile high-speed rail link connecting Spain and Morocco, facilitating travel between Europe and Africa.
  • China's $36 billion Bohai Strait Tunnel will become the world's longest underwater train route, spanning 76.4 miles.
  • Both projects support high-speed rail operations between 155 mph and 186 mph, significantly faster than the 99 mph limit of the English Channel Tunnel.
  • Deep-sea hydrostatic pressure and active seismic fault lines remain the primary limitations preventing faster construction timelines.

Project Timelines and Financial Costs

Funding for the Gibraltar Tunnel currently relies on initial grants distributed jointly by the European Union and the World Bank. Preliminary geological surveys alone cost $2.5 million in 2024, with the total construction budget projected to fall between $6.5 billion and $8 billion. Spanish and Moroccan officials aggressively target a partial operational opening by the 2030 FIFA World Cup, though independent civil engineering firms estimate a final completion date closer to 2038 due to the unprecedented complexities of deep-sea boring operations.

The economic viability of these multi-billion dollar projects depends entirely on securing high-volume freight subsidies alongside passenger ticket sales. High-speed passenger rail tickets for the Gibraltar crossing are projected to cost between $75 and $120, placing the service in direct competition with short-haul budget airlines operating out of Madrid and Casablanca. However, the true return on investment for both the Spanish and Moroccan governments stems from the dedicated freight corridors, which will bypass saturated maritime shipping lanes and heavily congested customs checkpoints at standard seaports.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the world's longest underwater high-speed train?

The world's longest planned underwater high-speed rail project is China's Bohai Strait Tunnel, spanning 76.4 miles (123 kilometers) connecting Dalian and Yantai. The Gibraltar Tunnel connects Europe and Africa but is significantly shorter at 24.2 miles.

How fast will the Gibraltar underwater train travel?

Passenger trains utilizing the Strait of Gibraltar tunnel are engineered to operate at maximum speeds of 186 mph (300 km/h), reducing the transit time between major European and African hubs.

Are underwater high-speed trains safe during earthquakes?

Engineers mitigate seismic risks by installing flexible tunnel lining joints and isolated track suspension systems designed to absorb tectonic shockwaves, though navigating fault lines like China's Tan-Lu zone remains a critical challenge.

How much will the Strait of Gibraltar tunnel cost to build?

The estimated construction budget for the Gibraltar tunnel is projected between $6.5 billion and $8 billion, supported by European Union infrastructure funds and World Bank grants.

Conclusion

The advancement of the Gibraltar and Bohai Strait tunnels marks a distinct transition in global infrastructure, shifting the logistics industry's reliance from maritime shipping vessels to high-speed subterranean rail. The $8 billion Gibraltar project provides a highly anticipated, physical link between Europe and Africa, facilitating immediate economic trade and rapid passenger transit without the logistical delays inherent to commercial ferry crossings. Concurrently, the $36 billion Bohai corridor establishes unprecedented length and depth records for underwater travel.

Investors and international logistics companies must monitor the geological survey results for the Gibraltar strait closely over the next 24 months. If modern boring technology successfully navigates the 1,550-foot depth requirements without severe budget overruns, it will validate the financial modeling for future intercontinental rail links worldwide. These projects are strictly engineered for high-volume freight and passenger corridors, providing a direct, low-emission alternative to short-haul aviation and reshaping continental supply chains.
