Deepfake Crisis 2026: AI Expert Reveals Why You Should Never Trust a Video Call Again — And What to Do About It

By Mike Reynolds Senior Editor
Published: June 01, 2026 Updated: June 01, 2026 12 Min Read

Imagine getting a video call from your elderly parent, frantic and crying, saying they've been arrested abroad and need £5,000 transferred immediately. You see their face. You hear their voice. You know it's them. You send the money. They were never on that call. What you saw was a real-time AI deepfake of your parent's face, their voice cloned from a few seconds of audio scraped from a Facebook video posted three years ago. By the time you speak to your actual parent and realise what happened, the money is gone and the fraudster is untraceable.

This isn't a hypothetical scenario from a dystopian film. It's happening right now. Multiple variants of this exact scam have been reported to UK Action Fraud and the FBI's IC3 in 2025 and 2026. And it's one of the more financially motivated uses of a technology whose broader applications are significantly more disturbing. Dr. Mhairi Aitken, formerly of The Alan Turing Institute and now leading Our AI Collective, has been documenting the state of deepfake technology and its social consequences. Her assessment is blunt: we are already past the point where the naked eye can reliably distinguish real from fake, and the implications for democracy, justice, and personal safety are severe.

What Deepfakes Are Now — Not What You Remember

The word "deepfake" entered public consciousness around 2017 when Reddit users started posting AI-generated face-swap videos. Those early examples were identifiable by anyone paying attention — the skin tones were wrong, the eye blinks were mistimed, the hair edges were blurry, the faces occasionally distorted when turning at angles. The phrase "wonky hands" became shorthand for obvious AI failure: early image generators couldn't produce realistic human hands, and that visual artifact became an amateur detection method.

None of that is true anymore. The generation technology has improved by multiple orders of magnitude. Current deepfake video generation — using tools like Runway Gen-3, Kling, and Sora — produces footage that human observers cannot reliably distinguish from real video in controlled testing. Academic studies measuring deepfake detection accuracy by untrained humans consistently find detection rates near chance level — around 50%. You might as well flip a coin.

More significantly, the barrier to creation has collapsed. Creating a convincing deepfake in 2017 required machine learning expertise, GPU hardware, and several hours of processing. In 2026, there are mobile apps — available on standard app stores until regulators force removal — that generate convincing face-swap videos in real time using a smartphone camera. You can point your phone camera at yourself, select a target person's face from an image, and appear on a video call as that person within seconds. The latency is low enough that it's usable in live calls.

Voice cloning has followed the same trajectory. Services like ElevenLabs, which has legitimate professional applications in audiobook production and accessibility tools, can generate an accurate voice clone from as little as three seconds of sample audio. A social media video. A voicemail. A short clip from a podcast. That's enough to create a voice model that can say anything its operators type.

Non-Consensual Deepfake Pornography — The Scale of the Crisis

The most numerically dominant use of deepfake technology is also the one that receives the least proportional coverage: non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII) created using AI. Sensity AI, a deepfake detection company that has been tracking deepfake content since 2018, has consistently found that over 90% of all deepfake video content online is non-consensual sexual imagery — fabricated pornographic footage depicting real, named individuals who did not consent to their likeness being used in this way.

The targets are not only celebrities. Early deepfake pornography focused disproportionately on high-profile women — actors, musicians, public figures with significant image libraries available for training. Taylor Swift's case in early 2024, when explicit AI-generated images of her spread across X (formerly Twitter) and reached tens of millions of views before platforms acted, became the highest-profile example and triggered legislative responses in the US. But the technology has democratised to the point where ordinary people — with limited public image libraries — are now regularly targeted.

The victim profile has shifted. Relationship abuse is a significant driver: ex-partners use deepfake tools to create and distribute intimate imagery as a form of control and harassment. Workplace harassment cases involving deepfake imagery have appeared in employment tribunal records in the UK. And schools are seeing deepfake imagery of students circulated among peer groups as a new form of bullying that carries the same psychological devastation as traditional intimate image abuse, with the added complication that the imagery is entirely synthetic — which some perpetrators have argued should reduce their culpability.

It doesn't. The psychological harm to victims of deepfake NCII is documented as being equivalent to that experienced by victims of real non-consensual intimate photography. The synthetic nature of the image does not reduce the humiliation, the loss of control over one's own image, or the social consequences of having explicit imagery circulate under one's name.

  • Deepfakes in 2026 are undetectable by the naked eye — human detection accuracy is at chance level (50%). Any video you see online, in a call, or shared in a message could now be fabricated. Over 90% of detected deepfake content is non-consensual sexual imagery targeting real people.
  • The Liar's Dividend — the ability to claim any genuine video is "AI-generated" — is now a real and documented defence strategy used by people caught on camera doing wrong. This threatens journalism, courts, and democratic accountability at a fundamental level.

Voice Clone Scams — When AI Sounds Like Your Family

Voice clone fraud is the deepfake threat that has most directly affected ordinary people's finances in 2025 and 2026. The mechanism is simple: a scammer creates a voice clone of a target's family member using publicly available audio — social media videos, voicemails, YouTube content — and then calls the family member in a manufactured emergency scenario.

The "grandparent scam" — where elderly people receive calls purportedly from a grandchild in legal trouble — existed before AI voice cloning, relying on impersonation and emotional manipulation. AI voice cloning transformed it from an occasionally convincing con to an almost indistinguishable impersonation. The cloned voice has the exact pitch, cadence, accent, and speech patterns of the real person. The emotional distress in the voice sounds genuine because AI models can generate emotional vocal characteristics as well as neutral speech.

The FTC reported over $25 million in losses from voice clone scams in the US in 2024 alone — and that figure represents only reported cases, with most victims not reporting due to embarrassment. UK Action Fraud saw a significant increase in AI voice fraud complaints in the same period. The FBI issued formal warnings specifically about AI voice fraud targeting families of military personnel deployed overseas — a specific vulnerability because families are primed to receive emergency calls and the deployed family member cannot easily be contacted to verify.

Business Email Compromise (BEC) scams have also incorporated voice and video cloning. In 2024, a finance employee at a multinational company in Hong Kong was tricked into transferring approximately HK$200 million (£20 million) after attending what appeared to be a video conference call with multiple colleagues, including the company's CFO. Every person on the call except the victim was a deepfake. The call was convincing enough that the employee only became suspicious after the transfers were complete and contacted the real CFO through a separate channel.

Deepfakes and Elections — How Democracy Is Being Targeted

The 2024 electoral cycle — which included US Presidential, UK General, and European Parliament elections among dozens of others globally — documented the first systematic use of political deepfakes at scale. The pattern is consistent: fabricated audio or video attributed to a real candidate saying something that will damage their campaign, released in the days immediately before an election when there is insufficient time for verification and correction to reach the full electorate.

The Slovakia election in September 2023 became the documented case study. Two days before the election, an audio recording circulated on social media purportedly featuring Michal Šimečka, leader of the liberal Progressive Slovakia party, discussing plans to buy votes and raise beer prices. Fact-checkers at AFP flagged the audio as AI-generated within 48 hours, but by then it had already been viewed millions of times and shared widely. Progressive Slovakia lost the election by a narrow margin. It is impossible to establish causal connection with certainty — but it is equally impossible to establish that the audio had no effect.

In January 2024, a robocall mimicking President Biden's voice was sent to New Hampshire voters ahead of the Democratic primary, telling them not to vote. The creator was identified and legal action followed — but the Biden voice clone was convincing enough that recipients reported initially believing the call was genuine.

Dr. Mhairi Aitken has identified the deepfake threat to elections as specifically acute because the damage mechanism doesn't require the deepfake to go completely undetected. The goal in political disinformation is rarely to persuade — it's to confuse, to create doubt, and to suppress enthusiasm and turnout. A deepfake that is debunked within 24 hours but reaches 20 million people before correction still achieves a disinformation effect.

The Liar's Dividend — When Real Evidence Can Be Faked Away

Legal scholars Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron introduced the concept of the "Liar's Dividend" in a 2019 paper that has since become foundational in discussions of deepfake policy. The concept describes an asymmetric harm: widespread awareness that deepfakes exist and are undetectable creates a situation where any genuine video evidence can be plausibly denied as AI-generated. The more convincing deepfakes become, the more credible the denial.

This is already being tested in legal contexts. Defence lawyers in criminal cases have raised AI generation as a reasonable doubt argument when clients are caught on video committing crimes. The argument doesn't need to succeed — it only needs to create sufficient doubt in a juror's mind. Some legal analysts believe that within five years, video evidence in criminal trials will require third-party forensic certification of authenticity before it can be considered probative — an expensive and time-consuming requirement that will disproportionately benefit defendants with resources.

For journalism, the Liar's Dividend creates a dangerous asymmetry. Publishing genuine investigative footage — a politician taking a bribe, a CEO defrauding shareholders, a police officer using excessive force — now invites an immediate "this is AI-generated" denial that will be circulated by supporters of the exposed party regardless of the evidence's authenticity. The burden of proof shifts onto the journalism rather than onto the subject's denial.

Dr. Aitken describes this as a threat to the epistemic foundations of democratic society — not because deepfakes will necessarily replace all truth, but because they systematically erode the conditions under which evidence-based reasoning can function. When any video might be fake, the psychological response for many people is not to become more cautious about all video — it's to trust only the videos that confirm existing beliefs and dismiss everything else as potentially fabricated.

Children and Schools — The Hidden Deepfake Abuse Crisis

The Internet Watch Foundation's 2025 annual report documented a significant increase in AI-generated child sexual abuse material (CSAM). Unlike earlier AI-generated CSAM which was relatively distinguishable from real imagery, current generation tools are producing content that requires specialist forensic analysis to identify as synthetic. The legal status of AI-generated CSAM is clearly established in the UK — the Protection of Children Act 1978 and subsequent legislation covers pseudo-photographs including AI-generated images — but detection and prosecution present practical challenges that law enforcement is still developing capacity to address.

In schools, the deepfake problem manifests differently but with serious consequences. Multiple documented cases across UK secondary schools in 2024 and 2025 involved students creating deepfake sexual imagery of classmates and circulating it within peer groups. The students involved in creation are often minors themselves, creating complex legal and safeguarding situations. The victims experience the same psychological harm as adult NCII victims but within a school social environment where the imagery's exposure is immediate and inescapable.

Ofsted and the Department for Education have issued guidance to schools, but the practical response — identifying that deepfake imagery exists, determining who created it, and managing both the victim's welfare and the creator's safeguarding needs — requires specialist expertise that most school pastoral teams don't have. The cases that make it to police and the courts represent a small fraction of incidents that schools are managing internally.

Smart Glasses and AR — The Next Deepfake Frontier

The current deepfake crisis primarily operates through screens — video calls, social media, messaging platforms. The next phase, enabled by widespread adoption of smart glasses and augmented reality headsets, moves deepfakes off screens and directly into the wearer's field of vision. Dr. Aitken identifies this as the feature of the deepfake problem that concerns her most in the medium term.

The scenario: you're wearing augmented reality glasses at a rally, a meeting, or a public space. The AI system processing your visual field replaces a real speaker's face and voice with a deepfake of someone else — or modifies what the real speaker appears to be saying in real time, replacing their actual words with fabricated ones. You are watching events happening in person in front of you, but what you see and hear has been altered without your knowledge.

Meta's Ray-Ban smart glasses, Apple's Vision Pro spatial headset, and the Android XR glasses announced at Google I/O 2026 are all platforms that process real-world visual input through AI systems. None of these products are currently being used for the kind of real-time visual manipulation described above — but the technical capability to do so exists and is not far from consumer accessibility. The security research community has begun documenting this as an attack surface that doesn't yet have established defences.

How to Spot a Deepfake in 2026 — What Still Works

Visual detection of deepfakes by eye is no longer a reliable primary method. Studies consistently find that even trained observers perform only marginally better than untrained ones when evaluating current-generation deepfake content. The following methods are what remains practical:

Verify context, not just content. Ask who is sharing this, why they are sharing it, and what their incentive is. A deepfake shared by a partisan account two days before an election, in a format that would be damaging if true, should be treated with significant scepticism regardless of its visual quality. Motive analysis remains effective even when visual analysis fails.

For live video calls: unexpected movement tests. Deepfake face replacement in real-time calls still struggles with sudden, unexpected movements. Ask the person to push their hand slowly toward the camera until it's very close. Ask them to tilt their head sharply to one side and then the other rapidly. Ask them to put their hand over their face and then remove it quickly. These sudden occlusions and angle changes are harder for real-time face replacement to handle smoothly. A lag, a visual glitch, or a brief moment of distortion is a warning sign.

The second-channel verification rule. For any call involving financial requests, security matters, or information you'd act on, establish a second-channel verification habit. If someone calls you claiming to be your bank, your employer, or a family member in trouble, hang up and call back on a number you independently look up or have on record. Do not use any number provided in the initial call.

Apply SIFT to news content. The SIFT method (Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims) developed by fact-check educators provides a practical framework. Stop before sharing or accepting content. Investigate who is responsible for the content. Find other coverage from multiple independent sources. Trace claims back to original sources rather than accepting shared secondary material.

Use detection tools where available. Tools like Hive Moderation's deepfake detector, Microsoft's Video Authenticator, and Reality Defender offer AI-generated content analysis. These tools are not infallible — they're in an arms race with generation tools — but they add a layer of analysis beyond unaided human judgment. Google's SynthID watermarking embeds invisible markers in AI-generated content from Google's own systems; detection of these markers, where present, provides high-confidence identification of synthetic origin.

UK and EU Laws on Deepfakes — What Protection Exists

The legal framework around deepfakes in the UK and EU has developed rapidly since 2022, though enforcement remains limited relative to the scale of the problem.

In the UK, the Online Safety Act 2023 includes specific provisions on non-consensual intimate deepfakes. Sharing intimate deepfake images without consent is a criminal offence under the Act, with provisions for prosecution even when the imagery is entirely synthetic. The Act also places duties on platforms to remove such content promptly upon notification.

The Criminal Justice Act 2024 introduced further provisions around intimate image abuse, expanding the range of sharing and creation behaviours that constitute criminal offences. Creating non-consensual intimate deepfake imagery — not merely sharing it — is addressed in more recent legislative proposals moving through Parliament in 2026.

In the EU, the AI Act classifies deepfake-generating systems as high-risk AI applications and requires transparency labelling: AI-generated content must be disclosed as such when there is a meaningful risk of deception. This provision comes into force progressively through 2025 and 2026. The Act also establishes prohibitions on certain AI applications including real-time facial recognition in public spaces and AI systems that manipulate people subliminally — both of which intersect with deepfake deployment.

The practical limitation of all this legislation is enforcement. Creating deepfake NCII and publishing it anonymously from a jurisdiction with no extradition agreement is still effectively consequence-free for most perpetrators. The platforms on which content appears are the more tractable enforcement point, and the OSA's platform duties represent the most practically enforceable element of the current legal framework.

What to Do If You're Targeted by a Deepfake

If you discover deepfake imagery or audio of yourself circulating without your consent, the response framework is the following:

Document before you report. Take screenshots of the content, URLs, timestamp information, and any associated account profiles before reporting to the platform. When platforms remove content upon report, the evidence disappears simultaneously. Documentation preserves it for any subsequent legal action.

Report to the platform immediately. All major platforms — Meta, X, TikTok, YouTube, Reddit — have specific reporting pathways for non-consensual intimate imagery, including AI-generated versions. The Online Safety Act creates legal obligations for UK-accessible platforms to respond promptly. Document your report with screenshots.

Contact Stop NCII. Stop NCII (stopncii.org) is a service run by the Revenge Porn Helpline and Internet Watch Foundation in the UK. It allows you to generate digital hashes of intimate imagery of yourself — without uploading the actual images — that are then shared with participating platforms to prevent the content from being uploaded or re-uploaded. This is specifically effective against organised distribution of intimate imagery across multiple platforms.

Contact the police. In the UK, sharing intimate deepfake imagery is a criminal offence. Report to your local police or use the Action Fraud online reporting tool (actionfraud.police.uk). Keep all documentation from step one for the police report.

Seek legal advice. Beyond criminal law remedies, civil actions for harassment, misuse of private information, or data protection violations may be available depending on your circumstances. Some law firms offer initial free consultations for NCII cases specifically.

Contact the Revenge Porn Helpline. The RPH (revengepornhelpline.org.uk) provides free, confidential support and practical assistance to victims of intimate image abuse, including AI-generated content. Their advisors have specific expertise in deepfake cases and can help navigate platform reporting, police reporting, and emotional support.

Is There Hope? What Regulation and Technology Can Do

Dr. Aitken's position — stated clearly and repeated consistently in her work — is that the current situation is serious but not irreversible, and that the response cannot be left either to individuals or to the companies that profit from the AI systems creating the problem. Effective response requires three components working together: regulation, technical tools, and public literacy.

Regulation needs to address both the creation and distribution sides of the deepfake problem, with particular urgency around non-consensual intimate imagery and political deepfakes. The UK's Online Safety Act represents a start, but the legislative response needs to stay ahead of the technical capability — which means ongoing updating rather than static law.

Technical tools — watermarking systems like Google's SynthID, AI detection tools, platform-level scanning for known deepfake signatures — are a partial solution. They face an inherent arms race with generation technology: as detection improves, generation adapts. But technical tools don't need to be perfect to be valuable. Even partial detection capability provides meaningful protection when combined with policy and literacy responses.

Public literacy may be the most durable long-term response. A population that approaches visual media with appropriate epistemic humility — not with blanket distrust of everything, but with practiced habits of verification — is significantly more resilient to deepfake manipulation than one that has been trained by decades of media to treat video as self-evidently authentic. The media literacy habits that protect against deepfakes are the same ones that protect against misinformation generally: source checking, second-channel verification, motive analysis, and the discipline to pause before sharing.

There are also societal signals worth noting. Dr. Aitken and other AI researchers have observed a growing counter-movement — people actively seeking out human-made content, creators who explicitly mark their work as entirely human-generated, communities forming around authentic human creativity as a value proposition precisely because AI-generated content has become so prevalent. The same market dynamics that created an audience for artisanal goods in response to industrial mass production may be creating demand for provably human creativity in response to AI-generated media saturation. That's not a solution to the deepfake problem, but it suggests that the cultural response is already forming alongside the technical and regulatory ones.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a deepfake?

A deepfake is AI-generated synthetic media — image, video, or audio — that realistically depicts a real person doing or saying something they never did. In 2026, deepfakes are indistinguishable from genuine footage to the naked eye and can be created in minutes using consumer tools accessible on smartphones.

Why are deepfakes more dangerous in 2026?

The technology is now undetectable without specialist tools, requires no technical expertise, and has scaled to tens of millions of pieces of content annually. Over 90% of detected deepfake content is non-consensual sexual imagery. Voice clones are enabling family fraud scams. Political deepfakes have influenced elections. And AI-generated CSAM is increasing at a rate that challenges law enforcement detection capacity.

What is the 'Liar's Dividend' and why is it terrifying?

The Liar's Dividend describes the scenario where the mere existence of convincing deepfakes allows anyone caught on genuine video to claim the footage is AI-generated. A corrupt politician, criminal, or abuser filmed doing wrong can now plausibly deny the authenticity of real evidence. This creates a fundamental threat to journalism, courts, and democratic accountability — because the more convincing deepfakes become, the more credible every denial becomes.

How can I spot a deepfake in 2026?

Visual inspection alone is no longer reliable. Practical steps: verify context and who is sharing it; for live video calls, ask for unexpected sudden movements that challenge real-time face replacement; always verify financial requests through a second independent channel; apply SIFT to news content; and use detection tools like Hive Moderation or Microsoft Video Authenticator for important content.

What laws protect people from deepfakes in the UK?

The Online Safety Act 2023 criminalises sharing non-consensual intimate deepfake imagery. The Criminal Justice Act 2024 expanded provisions around intimate image abuse. The EU AI Act requires transparency disclosure for AI-generated content. Enforcement remains limited, particularly for content created anonymously in foreign jurisdictions, but platform duties under the OSA are the most practically enforceable element.

What should I do if I'm targeted by a deepfake?

Document everything before reporting; report to the platform immediately; contact Stop NCII (stopncii.org) to prevent redistribution across platforms; report to the police (Action Fraud in the UK) as sharing intimate deepfakes is a criminal offence; contact the Revenge Porn Helpline for confidential support; and seek legal advice about potential civil remedies. Do not confront the creator directly.

Conclusion

We built a world where seeing was believing. Photographs proved things happened. Videos showed what people did and said. Courts admitted visual evidence. Journalism published footage. Families shared memories. The entire epistemology of modern society was built on the assumption that recorded media, while potentially edited or cherry-picked, captured something real that actually occurred.

Deepfake technology has broken that assumption. Not fully and not everywhere — we are still early enough in this transition that most people still treat video as broadly authentic and most content still is. But the assumption is broken in principle, and the damage is spreading. The Liar's Dividend is being deployed in courts. Voice clones are defrauding families. Schools are managing non-consensual intimate AI imagery of their students. Elections are being targeted with fabricated audio.

Dr. Aitken's refrain — "it's not too late" — is both a statement of fact and an instruction. The technical solutions exist. The regulatory frameworks are developing. The public awareness, while growing, is not yet at the level where people routinely apply verification habits to the content they consume and share. The window for building those habits, establishing those regulatory guardrails, and deploying those technical tools before the problem scales further is genuinely available right now. Whether it stays available depends on how quickly individuals, institutions, and governments take the threat seriously.
